ArtsEngine FEAST project · University of Michigan

Instruments that learn your movements.

TAMIE is building a platform where people with cognitive or motor disabilities train digital musical instruments to respond to the way they move — not the other way around.

  • Accessible music
  • Gesture & sensing
  • Machine learning
  • Inclusive design

About the project

For as long as people have been designing digital musical instruments, designers have been thinking about how to make these instruments accessible for people who may struggle to actively participate in music-making because of cognitive or motor constraints. Work on accessible digital musical instruments (ADMIs) has often been more creative in approach and scope than mainstream accessibility work — partly because it is not tied to keyboards, mice and desktop interaction paradigms, and partly because musical instruments already contain a wider array of sensors that can track a broader range of human movement.

Even so, people with disabilities have mostly been involved in ADMI design as end-users. TAMIE sets out not only to embody an inclusive design approach, but to build an environment where users themselves train their own instruments to respond to their movements.

Following the Third Paradigm in HCI — designing for the bodies of diverse users in diverse environments as the norm rather than the exception — TAMIE is developing a platform that combines sensing hardware, musical synthesis tools and an open, machine-learning-supported mapping layer that a user can use to map sensed action to musical output.

The approach takes advantage of a key property of musical instruments: a system needs to recognize all of the gestures of one player, all of the time — unlike general-purpose gesture recognition, which only needs to recognize everyone's gestures most of the time. As long as a player can reproduce a gesture consistently enough for a tunable classifier, an instrument can be configured to respond to their movements in ways that they themselves determine.

What we build on

  • Prior ADMIs Existing accessible instruments and their evaluation across the NIME, CHI and TEI literature.
  • Movement as material Research on bodily movement as a design resource for interactive systems.
  • Gesture learning Current AI and machine-learning work on gesture recognition, in HCI generally and DMI design in particular.

Where it's heading

Work produced with student and end-user participants will be shared at venues such as NIME, TEI and CHI. The project will conclude with a concert giving participants the opportunity to share their work and their art with the wider University of Michigan community. This site is where we share findings, progress and performances along the way.

The project draws on the Tech Suite in the Department of Performing Arts Technology, with prototype interfaces built in the workshop and lab meetings and participant workshops held in the fully accessible Davis Studio.
